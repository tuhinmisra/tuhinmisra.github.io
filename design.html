Aircraft Design

The mission comes first. The shape follows.

Aircraft design is the art of balancing demands that pull in opposite directions. I am drawn to it because no single choice is ever free. More range costs weight, more speed costs efficiency, and more payload costs agility.

New design and modification

I am equally interested in building something new and in adapting something that already exists. They reward different instincts.

Designing a new aircraft

A blank page allows the configuration itself to be chosen for the job. The work is to find the combination of wing, tail, and propulsion that meets the requirements with the least compromise.

  • Translate mission needs into measurable requirements
  • Select a configuration, such as conventional, flying wing, or blended body
  • Size the wing and propulsion system through constraint analysis
  • Verify stability, control, and performance before committing to hardware

Modifying an existing aircraft

An existing airframe brings proven structure and known behavior, but also limits. Success means adding the new capability while protecting the margins that made the original reliable.

  • Quantify the change in weight, balance, and drag
  • Reassess stability and control after every geometry change
  • Integrate new payloads, sensors, or propulsion with minimal structural impact
  • Confirm that performance in the new role justifies the modification

Designing for different missions

The same airframe can look excellent for one purpose and poor for another. These are the mission families I enjoy thinking about most.

Endurance and surveillance

Long, efficient wings, low wing loading, and careful weight control. The priority is time on station, so every gram and every unit of drag matters.

High aspect ratioEfficiency

High-speed flight

Thin sections, reduced wetted area, and attention to compressibility. At high Mach numbers, aerodynamics and propulsion become tightly linked.

Payload and utility

Volume, center of gravity range, and ease of loading drive the layout. A good utility aircraft stays predictable across a wide range of loadings.

Short field and flexible operations

High-lift devices, strong control authority at low speed, and rugged structure allow an aircraft to operate where runways are limited.

How I work through a design

Define the mission

Write down range, endurance, speed, payload, and operating environment before drawing anything.

Estimate and size

Use first-order methods to find a weight, wing area, and power level that close the design.

Analyze

Apply computational fluid dynamics and stability calculations to test whether the geometry behaves as expected.

Build and test

Fabricate, fly, and measure. Compare results with predictions and record every difference.

Iterate

Use what was learned to refine the design, or to adapt it to a new mission altogether.
